Don’t Stop Looking

Human first · Technology second

explore humanity first and technology afterward

First · look

People, places, things

  • Notice the world before you describe it
  • Talk with people; hear what matters
  • Read, walk, sketch, listen — slowly
  • Find the question that is worth asking
Then · build

Bring in the machine

  • Use AI to extend what you have seen
  • Let it draft, sketch, compose, code
  • Judge the result against the real thing
  • Then go back and look again

The tool can make almost anything. What is worth making comes from looking.

Before any tool: be human first. Look at people, nature, art, history — the material that gives a prompt something to say.

      A Short History of AI

      From rules people write to patterns machines learn · click a point to revisit it
      ❄ winter
      ❄ winter
      Symbolic AI

      Thinking as symbol manipulation

      The 1956 Dartmouth workshop (McCarthy, Minsky, Shannon, Rochester) named the field. Newell and Simon's Logic Theorist proved theorems from Principia Mathematica.

      Idea: intelligence = rules applied to symbols.

      • 1950Turing asks “Can machines think?”
      • 1956“Artificial intelligence” is named
      • 1958McCarthy creates LISP
      Logic

      Knowledge as statements, reasoning as proof

      Write facts and rules in formal logic; let the machine deduce what follows. Robinson's resolution principle made automated proof practical; Prolog made logic a programming language.

      Idea: if it's true, it can be proved.

      • 1965Resolution (Robinson)
      • 1972Prolog (Colmerauer, Kowalski)
      • 1974Funding dries up: the first “AI winter”
      Expert Systems

      Bottling an expert's know-how

      Knowledge engineers interviewed experts and wrote hundreds of IF–THEN rules. MYCIN recommended antibiotics; XCON configured DEC computers and saved millions. But rules were brittle and costly to maintain.

      Idea: expertise = many specific rules.

      • 1970sMYCIN (Stanford)
      • 1980XCON at Digital Equipment
      • 1987The market collapses: a second winter
      Neural Networks

      Learning from examples, not rules

      Artificial neurons add up weighted inputs. Instead of writing rules, you adjust weights until the network gets the examples right. Backpropagation (Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams) showed how to train many layers.

      Idea: knowledge lives in connection strengths.

      • 1943McCulloch–Pitts neuron
      • 1958Rosenblatt's perceptron
      • 1986Backpropagation popularized
      Deep Learning

      Many layers, much data, fast chips

      AlexNet won the 2012 ImageNet contest by a wide margin, trained on graphics processors (GPUs). Deep networks soon led in vision, speech and games.

      Idea: depth + data + compute.

      • 2012AlexNet
      • 2016AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol
      • 2018Turing Award: Bengio, Hinton, LeCun
      Transformers

      Attention is all you need

      A Google team introduced the transformer, which lets every word “attend” to every other word. It scales beautifully — and became the engine of language models.

      Idea: predict the next word, at enormous scale.

      • 2017“Attention Is All You Need”
      • 2018GPT and BERT
      • 2020GPT-3: 175 billion parameters
      Foundation Models

      One model, many tasks

      Trained once on vast text, images and code, then adapted to almost anything: chat, writing, coding, analysis, images, music and video. Today's models reason step by step and use tools.

      Idea: a general-purpose collaborator.

      • 2022ChatGPT launches
      • 2024Nobel Prizes for Hinton and Hassabis
      • 2026GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5.5, Gemini 4 Argon

      Symbolic: people write the rules.  →  Neural: machines learn the patterns.

      Seven builds, one per era. Brown dots are the symbolic tradition; teal dots are the neural one. Click any dot to go back to it.

      Note the two winters: hype, then disappointment. Worth remembering today.

      2024 Nobels: Hinton (Physics, with Hopfield) and Hassabis (Chemistry, with Jumper and Baker).

      How a Language Model Writes

      Temperature

      Low: safe, predictable choices. High: surprising ones — sometimes brilliant, sometimes nonsense.

      Real models work in tokens (word pieces, about ¾ of a word) and weigh tens of thousands of options each step.

      Fluent is not the same as true: the model predicts what sounds likely.

      Ask the room to guess the next word before pressing. Then switch temperature to High and run it again.

      1D · Knowledge Graph and Its Relatives

      Four ways to draw what we know

      Semantic network

      Ross Quillian, 1968

      Concepts as nodes; labeled links such as is-a and has. An early model of memory in AI.

      Concept map

      Joseph Novak, Cornell, 1970s

      Top-down, with linking phrases that read as sentences: plants — need — sunlight. Built for learning.

      Mind map

      Tony Buzan, 1974

      A central idea with radiating branches, color and images. Fast and personal; links are not labeled.

      Knowledge graph

      Google, 2012

      Facts as triples, subject – relation – object, at web scale. AI is very good at producing them.

      All four organize concepts around a topic. They differ in how strict the links are: labeled relations (semantic net, knowledge graph), readable propositions (concept map), or loose association (mind map).

      Guidelines

      Using AI honestly
      1 · Transparency

      Say if you used AI

      Always say whether and how AI helped: ideas, drafting, editing, images, music. Your readers deserve to know.

      2 · Copyright

      Respect others' work

      Don't feed in someone's work to copy their style or reproduce it. Check what you publish. Purely AI-made work may not be protected by copyright.

      Give credit
