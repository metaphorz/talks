Art ↔ Science

Subject ↔ subject · each one feeds the other

C. P. Snow called them “two cultures” that no longer talk (1959). They never stopped trading.

Art → Science

Galileo’s Moon, 1609

Thomas Harriot saw the Moon through a telescope first, and drew blotches. Galileo, trained in drawing light and shadow, saw mountains.

Science → Art

Seurat’s dots, 1884

Chevreul’s science of colour contrast (1839) taught Seurat to set pure colours side by side and let the eye mix them.

Art ↔ Science

Anna Atkins, 1843

Laid seaweed on light-sensitive paper to make cyanotypes: the first book illustrated with photographs, and a botanical record.

Art ↔ Science

Haeckel’s Art Forms in Nature, 1904

A zoologist’s plates of radiolarians and jellyfish became pattern books for Art Nouveau designers.

The idea in the middle: close observation. Both begin by looking hard at the world.

Snow's lecture, The Two Cultures (1959): scientists and literary intellectuals no longer understood each other. The history says otherwise.

[1] Galileo and Harriot looked at the same Moon in 1609. Harriot's drawing is blotchy; Galileo, who had trained in drawing, read the shifting shadows as mountains and craters. Art taught science to see.

[2] Seurat read Chevreul and the colour scientists. Science taught art to see.

[3] [4] Some work is both at once.

[5] Last week's form: A ↔ B through an idea. Here the idea in the middle is observation.

Humanities ↔ Engineering

Subject ↔ subject · what it is for, and how it stands up

Engineering asks how. The humanities ask why, and for whom.

Humanities → Engineering

Vitruvius, c. 20 BC

A building must have three things: firmness, usefulness and delight. Only the first is purely engineering.

Engineering → Humanities

The Brooklyn Bridge, 1883

Finished under Emily Warren Roebling after her husband fell ill. Hart Crane made it the centre of a book-length poem, The Bridge (1930).

Humanities → Engineering

“Do Artifacts Have Politics?”, 1980

Langdon Winner argued that machines and roads carry values: who they let in, and who they keep out.

Humanities ↔ Engineering

Technology with the liberal arts, 2011

Steve Jobs: “technology alone is not enough — it’s technology married with liberal arts, married with the humanities.”

The idea in the middle: human purpose. What we build says what we value.

[1] Vitruvius' three requirements: firmitas, utilitas, venustas. Firmness is engineering; usefulness and delight are about people.

[2] The Brooklyn Bridge: an engineering story that became a human one: Emily Warren Roebling running the work, then a poet's epic.

[3] Winner's essay: design decisions are value decisions.

[4] Jobs at the iPad 2 launch, March 2011.

[5] The idea in the middle here: purpose.

Art × Science

Can a drawing be evidence?Cajal drew neurons by hand; his drawings helped win a Nobel Prize (1906).

Can a lab make a colour?Prussian blue (c. 1706), the first modern synthetic pigment, came out of a Berlin lab.

Art × Engineering

Who chose the Golden Gate’s colour?Architect Irving Morrow: International Orange, to sit with the hills and show in fog.

Can a machine be a sculpture?Theo Jansen’s Strandbeests: plastic tubes that walk the beach on the wind.

Humanities × Science

Who gets asked for consent?Henrietta Lacks’s cells (1951) were taken without it, and fed decades of research.

Can a theory change who we think we are?Darwin’s Origin of Species (1859) did, in religion, philosophy and fiction.

Humanities × Engineering

Who is a design for?Curb cuts were won by wheelchair users; now strollers and suitcases use them too.

Can a machine change how we think?The printing press made reading private, and spread the Reformation.

Our two examples sit on the diagonal. The other two cells are pairs you might not have tried.

Fritz Zwicky: Swiss astronomer at Caltech, who also named dark matter and, with Walter Baade, supernovae. He developed the morphological box for jet-engine design in the 1940s: write down every dimension of a problem, list the options on each, and look at every combination, especially the ones nobody has tried.

Here both dimensions are subjects. Rows: Art, Humanities. Columns: Science, Engineering. The outlined diagonal holds today's two pairs; the box hands us two more.
