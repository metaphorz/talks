How It Began

Hurricane Andrew, failed insurers, and the Legislature’s answer
AUGUST 24, 1992

Hurricane Andrew

Category 5 at landfall, south Miami-Dade County
$15.5Binsured losses, the costliest U.S. disaster to that date

0standards for judging whether a hurricane loss model could be trusted
Losses: Property Claim Services. Insolvencies: A.M. Best (other counts range from 8 to 16).
1993

A state reinsurance fund

The Florida Hurricane Catastrophe Fund, s. 215.555, F.S., so a single storm cannot break the market again.

1995

The Commission

The Legislature enacts s. 627.0628, F.S. (CS/HB 2619, Ch. 95-276, Laws of Florida): a panel of experts to judge hurricane loss models.

1995

Tied to rates

Rate filings under s. 627.062, F.S. must use methods and models the Commission finds accurate or reliable.

1996

The audits begin

First standards adopted June 3, 1996. The Professional Team starts its on-site reviews, and the first model is found acceptable.

How it all began: Andrew made landfall in south Miami-Dade on August 24, 1992. Insured losses were about $15.5 billion; at least eleven insurers became insolvent. Nobody had a trusted way to price the risk.

Why Florida Built This

From a hurricane, to a statute, to a process

“Reliable projections of hurricane losses are necessary to assure that rates for residential insurance are neither excessive nor inadequate.”— FLORIDA LEGISLATURE, S. 627.0628(1), F.S. (1995)

Source: Hurricane Standards Report of Activities as of November 1, 2025 (Introduction; Overview).

The Florida Legislature wrote the statute, s. 627.0628, that created the Commission in 1995: a panel of experts to decide which hurricane models are accurate and reliable enough to use in setting residential insurance rates.

[builds] Andrew, the Cat Fund, the statute, the first standards and the first accepted model, the move to a two-year cycle, flood, and today's 2025 standards.

Who Does What

The parties in the process · hover a role

The Legislature created the Commission; the State Board of Administration houses it and runs the reviews.

[build 1] Modeling organizations build the models; the Professional Team audits them and reports to the Commission. Eleven experts in five disciplines; I am one of the two Computer/Information members.

[build 2] Insurers must use an accepted model in a rate filing; the Office of Insurance Regulation reviews the filing.

Anatomy of a Standard

G-1 · Scope of the Hurricane Model and Its Implementation

Standard

Commission · what must be true

A. The hurricane model shall project accurate and reliable loss costs and probable maximum loss levels for damage to insured residential property from hurricane events.
B. A documented process shall be maintained to ensure continual agreement and correct correspondence of databases, data files, and computer source code to presentation materials…

Purpose

why it exists

A high-level view of the model that projects loss costs and probable maximum loss levels for insured residential property.

Relevant forms

the numbers to supply

G-1M-1M-3M-4S-2A-1A-2A-3A-4A-5A-6A-8

Disclosures

modeling organization · in the Submission

6 disclosures, e.g. “Provide a fully labeled flowchart, concept map, or semantic network that illustrates interactions among, and the network organization of, major hurricane model components.”

Audit

Professional Team · on-site

5 audit items, e.g. “Compliance with the requirements in Standard G-1.B will be reviewed.” “Maps, databases, and data files relevant to the Submission will be reviewed as encountered.”

Every one of the 38 standards has the same layers. The Commission writes the standard; the modeling organization answers the disclosures in its Submission; the Professional Team works through the audit items on-site.

    What the Team Does On-Site

    The six parts of an on-site review · from the ROA
    1

    Due diligence

    Review what was submitted, focusing on changes since the last accepted model.

    2

    Live runs

    Run the model under the team’s control and supervision, in real time.

    3

    Data and code

    Inspect data and source code on request, in an agreeable time frame.

    4

    Verify the description

    Are the disclosures and forms valid, accurate and fairly complete?

    5

    Check compliance

    Standard by standard, audit item by audit item.

    6

    Trade secrets

    See proprietary data and methods that never appear in public.

    “The objective is to observe the hurricane model in operation and the results it produces during a ‘real time’ run. This is necessary in order to avoid the possibility that the modeling organization could recalibrate the hurricane model solely for producing desirable results.”

    At the end, every team member attests that all trade secret material and digital notes on their computers have been deleted, “and that the recycle bin or its equivalent has been emptied.”

    Pass, or Revisit

    The two outcomes of an on-site review
    The rule

    “The Professional Team may react to possible corrections proposed by the modeling organization but shall not tell the modeling organization how to correct the non-compliance.”

    If they disagree

    Two options

    Go to the Commission meeting and argue the case there, or withdraw and wait for the next standards.

    [build 1] Complies: the team reports that to the Commission.

    [build 2] Does not comply: fix it while we are there if possible; otherwise request an additional verification review within 7 days, send revised documents within 60 days, and the team (or part of it) comes back.

    [build 3] We audit; we do not consult.

    The Commission Decides

    A public meeting · every standard voted on
    1 · Public

    Overview and changes

    About 20 minutes; an hour for a new model.

    2 · Closed meeting

    Trade secrets

    Audit slides shown again. Recorded. No voting.

    3 · Public

    Standard by standard

    Each one stated, demonstrated with evidence, and voted on.

    Vote

    To be acceptable, a model must be found acceptable on every standard.

    “If the model fails to be found acceptable by a majority vote for any one standard, the model shall not be found acceptable.”

    Run both: all 38 pass, and the version where a single standard fails. A failed model can be appealed within 30 days.

    What Acceptance Unlocks

    The statute ties rate filings to accepted models

    “An insurer shall employ and may not modify or adjust actuarial methods, principles, standards, models, or output ranges found by the commission to be accurate or reliable in determining hurricane loss factors and probable maximum loss levels for use in a rate filing under s. 627.062. An insurer may employ a model in a rate filing until 120 days after the expiration of the commission’s acceptance of that model…”— S. 627.0628(3)(D), F.S.

    No accepted model, no hurricane rate filing. That is why modeling organizations go through all of this every two years.

    The same findings are used by the Cat Fund: the SBA must, “to the extent feasible,” use Commission-accepted methods to set FHCF reimbursement premiums (s. 627.0628(3)(c)).

    Follow the Money

    Premiums, the Florida Hurricane Catastrophe Fund, and its bonds
    Show

    Sources: FHCF annual reports and bonding capacity reports (fhcf.sbafla.com); s. 215.555, F.S.

    Quiet year: homeowners pay premiums; part of what the insurer collects goes to the Cat Fund as a mandatory reimbursement premium, priced with accepted models.

    After a major hurricane: the Fund reimburses insurers for part of their losses. If it needs more than it holds, it issues revenue bonds, repaid by emergency assessments on most Florida property and casualty policies, not only homeowners.
